Biography

I am a South-Argentinian academic researcher, composer, saxophonist, and sound artist based in Copenhagen.

me. I found through sound a way of knowing and an access to a world of data of many types. Throughout the years I developed as a musician and later as a researcher in connection to data and HCI, exploring the affective and entangled dimensions of human experiencing. I research how data can be felt rather than just read, turning perception itself into a research instrument. I further compose for ensembles, build interactive systems, and improvise on the saxophone. As a composer I created 10 records of long compositions navigating between contemporary music and jazz in my earlier career. My work received the Best Use of Sound Academic Award at ICAD 2024, among other recognitions.

‘Inspired by such people as Anthony Braxton, Miguel Crozzoli makes music that is both highly intellectual and emotional.’ — Jakob Baekgaard, allaboutjazz.com >more

research. I am a PhD fellow at the Intelligent Instruments Lab, University of Iceland, where I investigate how interactive multimodal systems can turn data exploration into an affective, embodied practice. I collaborate with people ranging from researchers to artists — including oceanographers, ecoacoustic experts, musicologists, and literary scholars studying emotions in the Icelandic sagas — developing meaningful data experiences for scientific dissemination and artistic contexts, treating data not as passive information but as an agent for meaning-making.

Flagship project · 2020–2022

Sounding Numbers — my SCI-Music Electronic-Acoustic Chamber Ensemble.

The music I composed for this group is inspired by climate emergencies and sounds like the spirits of nature rising in communion — like the sound of the ocean and the forest, weaving textural nets and spiking chants. The audience enters a space of reflection, where wild emotions emerge in a soulful journey.

After my big orchestral project The Strangement Society, I got curious about the creation of scientific sonifications — could I use my own aesthetic universe to design how sonifications should sound, and how different data mappings interact with sound parameters such as frequency modulation and panning?

During 2020–2022 I conducted Sounding Numbers, a practice-based art research project at the Rhythmic Music Conservatory in Copenhagen, part of my Advanced Postgraduate Degree. The data came from deforestation and ocean contamination — because I believe we should talk more about, and find new ways to communicate, climate change.

With my last piece, I Am The Ocean, came a plot twist: instead of scientific theories I worked from philosopher Timothy Morton's Hyperobjects — philosophy ecologies connecting to a more abstract layer of imagination.

The project had two artistic outcomes: my electronic-acoustic compositions and participatory sound installations — the sweet and harsh results of my perception of our dystopian present. A perception that shows the dark beauty of the end of the world, while offering awareness and hope for community action!

Orchestral project · 2018–2020

The Strangement Society.

My wild and beautiful SCI-Music Electronic-Acoustic Orchestra — a 13-piece ensemble with voice and electronics.

From 2018 until 2020 I composed music for this small orchestra, inspired by cosmology and in conversation with scientists conducting fascinating outer-space research. The musical outcome came as a journey into an immersive sci-fi experience: layers of textures driving hypnotic melodic passages that develop into beautiful choral layerings.

Three orchestral pieces, each grown from space science: neutron stars, colliding black holes, and the cosmic microwave background.

Collaboration

For Cosmic Transients I worked with Dr. Radoslaw Jan Wojtak of the DARK Cosmology Centre (Niels Bohr Institute, Copenhagen) — grounding the compositional process in the science, and the science in emotion.

Improviser

Freedom. Fire. Language. Communion.

Since I discovered my passion for playing music, I have been intrigued by the world of improvisation. I can easily track down the moment when I became fascinated by it, almost obsessed: it was a cassette, a present from my saxophone teacher in south Argentina — a cassette that contained the sweet fire of John Coltrane.

Throughout my career I learned different languages, traveling from jazz and free jazz to contemporary free improvisation. Every place I lived, toured, visited, and people I met gave me something new.

I am still in the quest for new sounds in spontaneous creations: besides my academic research and composition projects, I run different groups where I wonder and wander through the colorful paths of improvisation.

The Space We Hold Together

with Nicola Privato and Fabio De Pascale · Intelligent Instruments Lab × Science Communication Lab, University of Padova

An interactive sound installation on biodiversity loss. Up to five visitors wear bracelets and walk through the room; where they stand together steers a live prediction of biodiversity from 1961 to 2050 across six pressures (CO₂, deforestation, fishing, pollution, invasive species, population). Each visitor's position in the room weighs on a collective action, changing the prediction according to one another's embodied experience, seen through immersive visual projections and heard in dynamic soundscapes.

Sonic Weathering

with Florian Goeschke · the "Florianotron"

A shape-shifting sound sculpture that listens to its immediate environment. A small weather station (CO₂, temperature, humidity, light) feeds Dark Sonification, which slowly moves the motors of Florian's un:fold, a plywood jitterbug-tensegrity held by strings, and plays through transducers on its panels. As the body folds, string tensions shift and the whole object re-tunes: local weather heard as a wooden body that, over days, quietly inhales, detunes and re-settles.

Plant Data Worlds.

What becomes possible to perceive when we listen and sense more-than-human life through considered technology?

Ecological data and field recordings from living ecosystems become small, sounding, tactile sculptures: a three-dimensional signature of an inhabited space.

In the botanical greenhouse of the Garden School in Hveragerði, plant roots stretch, bacteria and insects make soil, bees pollinate, sunlight falls across the banana leaves; most of this life unfolds too slowly, too quietly, or at frequencies we aren't built to notice. Plant sensors and microphones gather its traces: growth rhythms, environmental changes, insect presence. Several machine learning algorithms shape the data into a unique digital form, which is then made into a small sculpture.

The data also gives each object its voice: a sound design derived from the harmonic structure of the data. The result is a data sculpture, held and felt, listened to as it voices itself, and navigated as a digital world, so that what was an abstract dataset becomes something to relate to with care.

A collaboration

Thomas Pausz (FR/IS), speculative designer and media artist, and Miguel Angel Crozzoli (AR/DK), sound artist and composer, artist-researchers at the Intelligent Instruments Lab, University of Iceland, working together since 2024.

Feedback Saxophone.

A bell-mounted speaker closes the tube, feeding the instrument's sounds and resonances back through itself.

The feedback saxophone, designed by Halldor Ulfarsson, uses a two-microphone configuration: one at the mouthpiece for frequency content, one at the bell capturing a chaotic, complex feedback signal. Closing different key combinations excites the feedback, while extended techniques such as multiphonics and circular breathing let the acoustic voice of the instrument interact with it.

Feedback Sensibilities.

A duo improvisation for feedback saxophone and a custom interface for feedback circuit sniffing, with Stefanos Vasilakis.

The saxophone relies on physical feedback: air, speaker and microphone form a closed acoustic loop inside the instrument. The circuit sniffing interface captures the electromagnetic field of circuit boards through coil inductors and processes it with algorithmic feedback (delay and waveguide mesh) into noise, clicks and resonances. Both improvisers first listen, then play, until the two instruments blur into one shared textural space.
